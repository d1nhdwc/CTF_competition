Abandoned hospitals and former sanatoriums tend to follow a surprisingly consistent life cycle: **they are built for a particular medical problem, medicine changes, the institution becomes obsolete, and then society has to decide what to do with a very large, highly specialized building.**

### Why did they become abandoned?

For **tuberculosis sanatoriums**, the biggest historical turning point was the development of effective drug treatments. Sanatorium medicine had relied heavily on rest, isolation, fresh air, sunlight and controlled living conditions. Purpose-built facilities were often located in mountains, forests, or coastal areas and designed around balconies, terraces, large windows and extensive grounds. citeturn0search1turn0search10

The arrival of antibiotics—particularly streptomycin after 1943—fundamentally changed TB treatment. Patients no longer needed to spend months or years isolated in specialized institutions, so the economic and medical rationale for enormous sanatorium complexes disappeared. citeturn0search21

But **medical obsolescence isn't the only reason hospitals get abandoned**. Common causes include:

- **Medical advances:** a disease becomes treatable elsewhere or a treatment model disappears.
- **Changes in healthcare organization:** hospitals consolidate into fewer, larger modern facilities.
- **Building standards:** old structures may no longer satisfy fire safety, accessibility, infection-control, energy, or technical requirements.
- **Location:** an isolated sanatorium may be badly positioned for modern healthcare.
- **Cost:** renovating a huge institutional complex can be more expensive than constructing a new facility.
- **Ownership and funding problems:** public institutions may close without having a realistic redevelopment plan.
- **Urban development:** valuable land can make demolition economically attractive.
- **Political or institutional restructuring:** particularly relevant to military, psychiatric, and state hospitals.

Research on abandoned healthcare facilities identifies the combination of changing medical practices, regulatory requirements, and relocation to newer buildings as major contributors to abandonment. citeturn0search3

### What happens afterward?

There are roughly **four common outcomes**.

**1. Continued medical use**

The simplest solution is to change the medical function rather than the building itself. A TB sanatorium might become a general hospital, rehabilitation center, nursing home, or another specialized healthcare facility.

For example, one Austrian lung sanatorium became a nursing home after advances in medicine reduced its TB patient population. citeturn0search11

**2. Adaptive reuse**

This is increasingly common with historically significant buildings. The structure is retained but given a completely different purpose:

- apartments
- hotels
- schools or universities
- offices
- cultural centers
- museums
- rehabilitation/wellness facilities
- tourism complexes
- community facilities

The architecture can actually make this attractive. Sanatoriums often have generous grounds, large windows, balconies, repeated rooms, and pavilion layouts that can be adapted to new functions. European architectural research describes former sanatoriums as an important part of 20th-century architectural heritage precisely because of these qualities. citeturn0search2

A particularly interesting example is the former Terrassa Sanatorium in Spain: it remained in healthcare for a period before eventually becoming an audiovisual/film-related complex. citeturn0search16

**3. Heritage preservation**

Some buildings are preserved primarily because their historical or architectural significance has become greater than their practical usefulness.

That can mean anything from a fully restored functioning building to a partially restored complex where only important structures are protected. Sometimes the surrounding landscape is treated as part of the heritage too—the architecture, gardens, paths, terraces and relationship with the natural environment collectively tell the story of the institution. Research on the enormous Sondalo sanatorium complex in the Italian Alps, for example, treats the entire "sanatorium village" as heritage rather than simply one building. citeturn0search0

**4. Demolition or controlled decay**

Preservation isn't always economically or technically feasible. Buildings can deteriorate to the point where stabilizing them is extremely expensive, while later additions may have little architectural significance.

Consequently, redevelopment sometimes involves **keeping the historically important core while demolishing less significant structures**. citeturn0search3

At the other extreme, some sites are deliberately allowed to become ruins. The former **Beelitz sanatorium complex near Berlin** is an interesting modern example: parts of the complex were abandoned in 1994 and subsequently incorporated into a recreational landscape, with researchers examining the idea of "curated decay" rather than attempting to return every building to its original state. citeturn0search13

### Why do so many of them *look* abandoned?

There's an important distinction between **closure and abandonment**.

A hospital can close but immediately enter a redevelopment process. Another might sit empty for decades because ownership, financing, heritage restrictions and planning regulations make intervention difficult. During that period, roofs leak, windows break, vegetation takes over, vandals enter, and structural deterioration accelerates.

That's why an apparently straightforward question—*"Why don't they just renovate it?"*—can have a complicated answer. A huge hospital may contain hundreds of rooms and extensive infrastructure while its original layout is fundamentally incompatible with modern requirements. citeturn0search4

There's also a fascinating historical paradox: **the very features that made sanatoriums effective symbols of their era can make them difficult to reuse today.** They were deliberately isolated, spacious, pavilion-like institutions surrounded by nature. Modern healthcare generally favors accessibility, compact infrastructure, advanced equipment and integration with wider healthcare networks.

### And there's a particularly interesting Croatian example

The former **Krvavica Children's Health Resort near Makarska** illustrates this history unusually well. It was designed by architect Rikard Marasović in the mid-1960s as a modernist sanatorium for children with respiratory diseases. The complex subsequently went through several uses before being abandoned early in the 21st century and left to deteriorate. Recent Croatian architectural scholarship treats it specifically as a question of **modernist architectural heritage, adaptation and repurposing**. citeturn0search9

So the story of these places isn't simply *"hospital → abandoned building."* It's more like:

**medical problem → specialized institution → medical breakthrough → obsolete function → closure → competing possibilities of reuse, preservation, redevelopment or demolition.**

And that's why abandoned sanatoriums are particularly interesting historically: **the building itself is often a physical record of an entire medical philosophy that no longer exists.**
